Collective Image Lab

The same images, read more than one way.

A browser tool for looking at a room’s worth of images together. Participants load their own images, arrange them by visual similarity, by image content or by hue, and open published collections built by earlier sessions. Everything runs in the browser; the page uploads nothing.

01 · Architecture

What is hosted where

The Lab and Collections interface is hosted by GitHub Pages. Participants use Lab to load and arrange images in their own browser. Published collections are stored as JSON files in the same repository and explored through Collections.

Nothing about this is server-side. Layouts are computed on the participant’s own machine each time they arrange, which is why the arrangement is something they perform rather than a result handed to them.

Images a participant loads into Lab never leave their browser. The page makes no upload of its own. Contributing to a shared collection is a separate, deliberate act — see Saving and contributing.

The one thing fetched from outside the site is the small pretrained model used by the Content arrangement, downloaded once per session from a public CDN. It is the model that travels to the images, not the images to the model.

Opening the tool lands on a full-screen welcome image. Click the crop-mark lockup — or tab to it and press Enter — to go through to the workspace. The animation there is decorative and stands down for anyone whose system asks for reduced motion.

02 · The interface

Lab and Collections

Two views, switched from the buttons at the right of the header. They answer different questions, and the interface colours them differently so it stays clear which set of images is on screen.

Lab

A participant’s own images, held in their browser for the session and gone when the tab closes.

1 Your images → 2 Arrange → 3 Record & contribute

Collections

Published sets built from images earlier groups chose to contribute. Open to anyone with the link.

1 Collections → 2 Arrange → 3 Record

The left rail always shows the same three numbered steps, and step 2 stays greyed out until there are images to work with. Both views feed the same canvas: whatever is loaded, from either source, is arranged by the same methods.

Loading your own images

Drop files or a whole folder anywhere on the page, or use Choose images and Choose folder. Dropped folders are read through their subfolders, and files are ordered by name before loading, so a numbered set stays in its own order. A progress bar runs while images are read, and the line beneath it reports how many were added and how many are now on the canvas.

Anything the browser cannot use is skipped rather than stopping the load. A skipped files panel opens with one line per file and the reason: unsupported type, undecodable, or past the 500-image canvas limit.

03 · Sequence

Running a session

A workable shape for a session, whether you are running one or working through it on your own. The colour of each marker tells you whose images are on screen at that point.

The participant’s own images
A published, shared collection
  1. Everyone generates or gathers images Whatever the brief is — a prompt run through an image model, a set found in the wild, a personal archive. Ask participants to keep the files together in one folder.
  2. Open Lab and load them Drag the folder into Your images, or use the file picker. Nothing is uploaded.
  3. Arrange, and arrange again Run Similarity, then Content, then Hue, on the same set. The gaps between the three arrangements are usually where the discussion starts.
  4. Compare across the room Participants who ran near-identical briefs rarely get near-identical arrangements. Put two screens side by side.
  5. Open a published collection Switch to Collections and choose a set by name. Now the same arrangements run over hundreds of images from other groups — or over a collection with your own images mixed into it.
  6. Keep the evidence Save view for the picture, Save layout for the coordinates. Two arrangements of one set, saved side by side, are what a write-up can actually argue from.
  7. Close on what the arrangement can and cannot see Hue reads colour. Similarity reads colour and composition. Content reads a pretrained model’s sense of subject matter. None of them reads intent or provenance — which is the point worth landing.

04 · Arranging

The three arrangements

Choose a method in step 2, then press Run. Each run is computed from scratch on the machine in front of you; the status box at the bottom left counts it through, and a line at the bottom right names the arrangement currently on screen.

Similarity

Describes each image by colour and layout — a 12×12 grid of pixel values plus a 24-bin hue histogram — and maps those descriptions with t-SNE, which pulls images with similar descriptions into neighbourhoods.

Up to 250 images

Content

Passes each thumbnail through MobileNet, a small pretrained network, and uses the layer just before its final guess — roughly a thousand numbers describing what the network noticed — as the input to the same t-SNE.

Up to 250 images · first run needs the network

Hue

Sorts images by average hue, ties broken by lightness, and lays them out as an ordered contact sheet. No clustering, no randomness: the same set gives the same sheet every time.

Up to 500 images

Similaritycolour & layout features → t-SNE → 2D neighbourhoods → spacing
ContentMobileNet embedding → t-SNE → 2D neighbourhoods → spacing
Hueaverage hue → sorted grid

Why Similarity and Content disagree

The productive comparison is between these two. A portrait shot against green foliage and a photograph of a forest can land next to each other under Similarity, which never looks past colour. Under Content they usually separate, because the representation is closer to what is pictured than to what colour it is; two portraits under quite different lighting are more likely to become neighbours instead. Running both on one set, and asking which pairs survive the switch, makes the difference between the two notions of “alike” concrete.

What to expect from each run

Similarity and Content are stochastic: they will not come out identical twice, and re-running is a legitimate way to see which groupings are stable and which were an accident of one run. Hue is deterministic. After t-SNE finishes, a collision-spacing step pushes overlapping thumbnails apart so they can be inspected; it moves them on screen without changing the similarity that was computed.

The first time Content runs in a session it downloads the model (a few megabytes) and reads every image through it, so it takes noticeably longer than later runs. That first run needs a working connection; after it, everything is local again. If the download fails, the status box says so and Similarity and Hue carry on working.

Above 250 images, Similarity and Content are switched off. The buttons disable themselves, an amber notice explains that exact t-SNE does not stretch that far, and the tool falls back to Hue. If a session needs Similarity or Content on a large set, split the images into smaller batches rather than trying to force it.

All three arrangements are calculated from a representative static frame. They analyse appearance, not motion, so an animated GIF is read on the strength of one frame even though it stays animated on screen.

05 · The canvas

Reading the canvas

The arrangement is not a finished picture to be received; it is something to move around in. Thumbnails ease into position, and the view can be pushed well past the point where the whole set is visible at once.

GestureWhat it does
Scroll / pinch Zooms about the pointer, from the whole canvas down to a single thumbnail
DragPans the view
Click a thumbnail Opens it large, with its filename, whether it is yours or from a collection, and its position in the set
Click againCloses the enlarged image

Every thumbnail carries a coloured border: violet for an image the participant loaded, azure for one belonging to a published collection. On a mixed canvas that border is what lets you see how your own images distribute themselves through someone else’s set — often the most telling thing on screen.

Clear canvas, at the foot of step 1, empties everything and returns the tool to its starting state.

06 · Limits

Supported images and capacity

The recommended maximum is 500 images per collection or canvas. JPEG, PNG, WebP and GIF files are accepted, along with anything else the browser can decode as an image. GIF animations are preserved and remain visibly animated in Lab and Collections.

ViewCeilingAbove the ceiling
Similarity250 Disabled; the interface directs users to Hue
Content250 Disabled; the interface directs users to Hue
Hue500—
Collection or canvas500 Loading stops at the limit and says so
  • Unreadable or unsupported files are skipped rather than stopping the whole load, and their filenames are reported.
  • Large collections may be divided into multiple JSON parts, kept below GitHub’s browser-upload limit; the tool loads the parts in sequence.
  • Very large sets take a moment to arrange. Keep the tab in the foreground while a layout is computing.
  • Because a collection and your own images share the 500-image canvas, loading a large collection first leaves little room for your own. Load yours, then add a collection, if the comparison is the point.

07 · Shared sets

Opening a collection

Switch to Collections and open the picker in step 1. Each entry gives the collection’s name, how many images it holds and how large it is; the line above the picker reports the archive as a whole. Collections are ordered by creation time, newest first, and named after the images or the seminar theme rather than a date or a class time, so a set stays findable long after the session that made it.

A collection never loads automatically. The participant chooses which set to open, and it loads into whatever is already on the canvas rather than replacing it — which is how a group’s own images end up arranged among a published set.

Once the archive passes six collections, a search field appears at the top of the picker. A collection already on the canvas is tagged loaded and cannot be added twice.

Some collections are tagged locked in amber. Locking closes a collection to new images; it does not close it to you. A locked collection loads, arranges and exports exactly like any other.

The tool reads its archive from an index file, so a different archive can be opened by adding ?collection=…/index.json to the address — useful for a module that keeps its own set of collections alongside this one.

08 · Keeping and contributing

Saving and contributing

Step 3 appears once an arrangement has run. It holds two exports and, in Lab, the route into the shared archive.

Save view

A PNG of the canvas exactly as it is on screen, named after the arrangement. Frame the view first — zoom and position are captured as they stand.

Save layout

A CSV of the arrangement: one row per image with its position as x and y between 0 and 1, whether it is yours or from a collection, and its filename. Enough to rebuild or re-plot the arrangement elsewhere.

Saving the layout alongside the picture is what makes a run citable. Two CSVs from Similarity and Content over the same set can be compared directly, whereas two screenshots can only be described.

Contributing images

Contributing is separate from the lab exercise. Every participant completes the exercise whether or not they contribute images to the shared archive, and it is worth saying so out loud before anyone submits anything.

The Donate your images button opens a submission form in a new tab, where original files are sent deliberately and by hand. Nothing is uploaded by the page itself, at any point. Where a locked collection is selected, the button is replaced by a note explaining that no further images can be added to it.

Images are contributed under CC BY 4.0, with attribution to the module and department that ran the session. To have an image removed, contact the module convenor: it is withdrawn at the next rebuild, and its position is not reused.

09 · Publishing

Getting a collection published

Turning a folder of contributed images into a published collection is done with a separate, access-controlled builder, and the resulting files are committed to the repository by hand. That step is not open to everyone.

If you have run a session and want its images published as a collection, contact the module convenor. You do not need builder access to run a lab — everything in this tutorial works with the public tool alone.
